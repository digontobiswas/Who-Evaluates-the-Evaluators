Only \num{RetainedCount} of six families---\num{RetainedFamilies}---pass (\cref{tab:monotonicity}),
both on all \num{MonoBases} base policies.
The four perception and braking families fail even in closed loop: blanking one camera costs LTF a
large drop, but blanking more barely changes the score; image noise \emph{raises} TransFuser's score
at low levels; stale observations and late braking are not monotone.
Popular corruption benchmarks therefore assume an ordering that the closed-loop reference does not
confirm, and we do not use them as ground truth.

On the disjoint measurement scenes the retained orders hold: BridgeSim recovers the full order of
both families for TF, LTF and the held-out DD (Kendall $\tau=1$, $P_\text{full}\ge0.98$).

With all measurement scenes every evaluator recovers both orders perfectly, so we compare sample
efficiency (\cref{tab:efficiency}, \cref{fig:efficiency}).
On latency, the timing failure, closed loop needs \num{EffLatencyBridgesim} scenes against
\num{EffLatencyNavsimVone} for NAVSIM v1 ($P_\text{full}$ at 100 scenes \num{PfullLatencyBridgesim}
vs.\ \num{PfullLatencyNavsimVone}).
On lateral noise the two are equal (\num{EffLateralBridgesim} scenes).
Open-loop L2 is the most sample-efficient on both (\num{EffLatencyLtwo} scenes): it measures the plan
directly, and both families change the plan.
Our pre-registered hypothesis H1, that closed loop orders timing degradations better than the open-loop
and non-reactive evaluators, is therefore only partly supported: closed loop beats NAVSIM on latency
but not open-loop L2.
Read alone, sensitivity would crown L2 the best evaluator.
The specificity test shows why it is not.

Both benign families are validated on the selection scenes: for every base and level the 90\%
interval of the closed-loop drop lies within one DS point.
On the measurement scenes BridgeSim's largest benign-to-harmful ratio is \num{SpecRatioBridgesim}
(\cref{fig:specificity}); its level-3 drops lie between \num{TailDropMinBridgesim} and
\num{TailDropMaxBridgesim} DS points (\cref{tab:specificity}).
NAVSIM v1 loses \num{TailDropMinNavsimVone}--\num{TailDropMaxNavsimVone} PDMS points for the same
perturbations, \num{SpecPctNavsimVone} of what it charges for the harmful version; NAVSIM v2 behaves
the same (\num{SpecPctNavsimVtwo}).
NAVSIM simulates the whole four-second plan without replanning, so it scores plan content that a
replanning vehicle discards.
Open-loop L2 also penalises the benign edits, by \num{SpecPctLtwo} of its harmful penalty: it is the
most sensitive evaluator and not a specific one.
The verdicts hold for DD, which was never used to design or validate the perturbations, and for tail
stretch (NAVSIM v1 up to \num{StretchPctNavsimVone}, L2 \num{StretchPctLtwo} of the harmful penalty).
We treat tail stretch as secondary evidence: its harmful twin, late braking, barely moves the
closed-loop score itself (\cref{tab:monotonicity}).

\Cref{tab:robustness} repeats the selection under protocols that split the \num{NavhardScenarios}
NavHard scenes into a selection and an evaluation half instead of using disjoint scene sets.
With half the scenes and all six bases, no family passes: latency misses one step for one base
(\num{SplitWorstLatencyBase}, $P=\num{SplitWorstLatencyP}$, in the expected direction) because the
intersection--union rule becomes stricter with every added base while halving the scenes reduces power.
On seed~0 only, latency passes.
The benign validation fails on the half because one interval exceeds the one-point margin, not because
of a significant drop.
These analyses motivated the disjoint-scene design of our main analysis, which keeps every selection
scene; we report them in full.
Under BridgeSim's EPDMS, which ignores progress, no family passes on the half.
